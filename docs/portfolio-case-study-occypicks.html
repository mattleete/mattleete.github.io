The Challenge
Every World Cup, a group of friends wanted a fun way to compete over the tournament — but traditional fantasy games are player-by-player, high-effort, and overwhelming.
The Approach
Design and build something simpler and more social: a live snake draft of all 48 teams and a tiered scoring engine that keeps every match meaningful — shipped as a real product.
The Outcome
23 friends played through the entire 2026 World Cup at occypicks.com — a live draft, a season-long leaderboard, and a champion decided on the final kicks.
01

The Idea

Every four years my friends want in on the World Cup — but the usual fantasy formats ask you to manage a squad of individual players, track line-ups, and make weekly transfers. It’s a lot of admin for a bit of fun, and it rewards whoever has the most time rather than the most nerve.

Occypicks started from a simpler idea: draft whole teams, not players. Everyone drafts a couple of nations, then lives and dies by how far they go. One decision, made once, that pays out over a month of football — low effort to play, high stakes to watch.

03

Designing the Game

The design problem wasn’t really the interface — it was the rules of the game. A good format had to be effortless to join, fair for everyone regardless of football knowledge, and gripping right up to the final whistle. Four principles shaped every decision:

Teams, not players
Draft nations, not squads. One decision that pays out over a month — minimal admin, maximum investment.
Every match matters
A tiered multiplier (×1 to ×4) means underdogs score more per point than favourites, so a minnow’s run is worth as much as a giant’s.
The draft is the event
The live, real-time draft room is the social centrepiece — everyone in the same room, picks landing instantly, banter guaranteed.
Zero friction to play
Magic-link login (no passwords), a clean set of tabs, and sensible defaults, so anyone can join in a couple of taps.
The scoring model — a tiered multiplier (×1 to ×4) makes underdog wins worth more, keeping every match meaningful.
04

Building It

A live multiplayer draft is deceptively hard. Getting it right meant solving three problems most fantasy tools don’t: making it feel instant, making it impossible to cheat, and making sure it never stalls.

Instant, everywhere
In the draft room, every pick appears on everyone’s screen within a few hundred milliseconds over WebSockets — so a remote draft feels like being in the same room.
Fair by construction
Turn order and “you can’t take a team someone already has” are enforced on the server, not the client — so the game can’t be gamed, even by someone poking at the code.
Never stalls
Miss your turn and a drag-and-drop preference list lets the system auto-pick your highest-ranked available team, keeping the draft moving.
A living scoreboard
A scoring engine merges a live match-results feed with manual overrides. When the data API’s tight rate limit threatened to break at scale, I moved fetching to a scheduled job that caches results — so it works for one viewer or a hundred.
The hard part was never the football — it was making a multiplayer system feel instant and impossible to cheat, with one person building all of it.

The title came down to the wire: the champion took it on 57 points — despite both her teams being knocked out by the Round of 16 — edging me into second by a single penalty-shootout result at the ×4 multiplier. Crucially, the scoring design held up: average points per team came out remarkably even across all four multiplier tiers — exactly the competitive balance I’d been designing for.

06

Reflection

Shipping solo means owning every decision.
Designing and building it myself meant no handoffs and nowhere to hide — every rough edge was mine to fix. The product was better for the tight loop between design intent and implementation; I could feel a bad interaction and fix it in the same sitting.
The most interesting design problem was trust, not pixels.
People needed to believe the draft couldn’t be rigged. That fairness requirement pushed real rules onto the server and shaped the whole architecture — a reminder that some of the most important design decisions live well below the surface of the UI.
The best usability test is people who care.
Watching 23 friends actually use it — argue over picks, check the table daily, celebrate a minnow’s upset — taught me more than any lab session, because the stakes were real to them.
