## Young members were leaving just as their balances started to matter

The fund had a known retention problem with its younger members. Many of them had joined through a first casual or part-time job, had never made a deliberate choice about their super, and as their careers and balances grew they started to feel they had outgrown the fund. The product itself was competitive, so the issue sat in the experience, which was inconsistent across channels and gave members very little reason to feel confident they were in the right place.

The commercial cost was that the fund was losing members at the exact moment they were about to become its most valuable, and the loss compounds, because a member who leaves in their twenties takes decades of contributions with them.

## Starting from the fund's research instead of commissioning more

The fund had already invested in qualitative and quantitative member research, and we had twelve weeks. We chose to work from that body of research rather than run our own discovery, which gave us most of the engagement for strategy and design, and meant accepting that we would be working from someone else's questions.

To manage that risk, we ran a workshop with the fund's strategy and product teams early on to test our synthesis against what they knew from operating the business. The output of this phase was a definition of the at-risk segment, a map of the moments where the experience was falling short, and a first set of design principles.

## App logins gave the work a measurable target

One finding from the fund's data shaped the rest of the engagement. Members who logged into the app churned at a markedly lower rate than those who didn't, which made app engagement one of the strongest behavioural signals of retention the fund had.

I used that to anchor the concept work. A better experience is hard to hold a team to, whereas a member opening the app for the first time, and then coming back, is something the fund could measure. It also meant being deliberate about what we left out, and concepts that improved the experience without giving members a reason to open the app were ranked lower.

## A vision and three principles to hold every concept against

We set the vision as "make super surprisingly simple". Super is complex and we weren't going to change that, so the aim was for engaging with it to feel easy, and occasionally better than members expected. Three principles sat under the vision: intuitive, so nothing needed explaining; personalised to the member's goals, life stage and financial literacy; and surprising, meaning small moments of value in places members didn't expect them.

I facilitated discovery and ideation workshops with up to 20 of the fund's stakeholders, and we built the current and future state journey maps with their subject matter experts in the room. That took longer than mapping it ourselves, but the fund's teams needed to own the framework after we left, and the easiest way to get there was for them to have built it.

## Three journeys, weighted towards the job change

We mapped three journeys: acquiring and onboarding a member, giving them reasons to stay, and the job change. The job change was the highest-risk moment. Many young members didn't know they could take their super with them to a new employer, and the experience at that point had the least to offer them, so it carried the most weight in the concept work.

We ranked three concepts highest, one for each journey. "Your super so far" replaced the generic first-login dashboard with a summary of what had already built up on the member's behalf, which answered whether the app was worth their time without needing any financial literacy. Life milestone triggers reached members at moments the fund could reasonably infer, such as starting a new job, with one short and relevant thing worth knowing about their super at that point. "Just one thing" surfaced a single action or piece of information each time a member opened the app, so that no visit felt like homework.

## Testing dropped a concept the team had rated highly

We designed the concepts in Figma at a fidelity that felt real to members but was still quick to change, and tested them with members from the at-risk segment. The testing focused on comprehension and relevance, and on whether members felt the concepts were a reason to stay.

Several concepts were reworked as a result. One that had done well in our internal reviews was substantially de-prioritised, because testing showed it solved a problem members didn't actually experience as painful. It was a concept the team liked, and dropping it was the right call, since the alternative was handing the fund a well-argued concept for a problem its members didn't have.

## Prioritised on the roadmap, and a return engagement

The final deliverables were the CX framework, the journey maps and the design concepts as high-fidelity Figma prototypes. The fund prioritised the chosen concepts in its product roadmap, and came back to us for further CX and UX engagements.

The most useful thing the framework did, in my opinion, was make the inconsistency visible to the people who ran the product. Each touchpoint had been designed by different teams at different times and each one was reasonable on its own, and it was only laid end to end in a journey map that the fund's teams could see what a member was actually experiencing.

## The persona stopped doing work halfway through

We built a persona that showed one member at two ages, 18 and 21, to capture how their relationship with super changed as their life did. It was very useful for aligning the fund's team on who we were designing for early on. As the work became more touchpoint-focused it turned into a reference point rather than a design tool, and if I ran this again I would anchor each concept back to the persona's specific goals and anxieties at each stage, which I think would have produced sharper and more differentiated concepts.
