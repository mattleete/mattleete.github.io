## Mindfulness is hard to start, and hard to study

Mindfulness has well-documented benefits, but beginners often find it hard to get started with traditional practices such as seated meditation on their own, which left room for new practices that are easier for novices to start with. Designing one raised a second problem. The experience is individual and internal, which makes it hard to capture with traditional data collection, and asking people to criticise an experience cuts against the practice itself.

This was my Master of Interaction Design research project at the University of Sydney, supervised by Karen Cochrane. I led the research. I formed the research question and hypothesis, designed the research parameters, built the system and set up the tech stack, ran the sessions, and synthesised the findings.

## Turning brainwaves into a shared soundscape

Sound of Mind was an interactive sound installation for group walking meditation. Participants wore a Muse EEG headset, and their brainwave patterns modulated a soundscape I built in Max/MSP and Ableton Live, with the aim of encouraging harmony between body and mind while the group walked together.

## Adding body scans so participants could map what they felt

I redesigned the session to add a second mindfulness practice, a body scan, at the beginning, middle and end of the walk. The body scans asked participants to notice their experience at each stage, and that made it possible to use body mapping, an experimental qualitative method where participants draw what they felt onto a stylised outline of the body. We then discussed each person's body maps in a focus group after the session. We ran two sessions with 8–10 participants each.

## An experience timeline from the start of the practice to the end

Thematic analysis of the body maps and the focus group discussions gave us a much clearer picture of the experience, and a framework for designing similar experiences in future. It also produced experience timelines that model how the experience changes across the practice for several personas. The general timeline covers four areas: arousal, attention, self-regulation and immersion.

## What I'd change: designing the lead-up to the session

Redesigning the session around the body scans worked for both sides. It gave participants a better mindfulness experience, and it gave us the data the research needed. If I ran the research again, I'd design the lead-up to the session as well. Participants told us they brought their day into the practice, and those who arrived stressed found it harder to engage, so a calmer lead-up would likely improve the experience and make the results more consistent.

## Published at MOCO '20 and TEI '21

Karen Cochrane wrote up the research in two peer-reviewed papers, and I contributed as a co-author. I presented the work at the International Conference on Movement and Computing (MOCO '20), and the second paper was published at the International Conference on Tangible, Embedded, and Embodied Interaction (TEI '21).

- Cochrane, Loke, Campbell, Leete and Ahmadpour. [An Interactive Soundscape to Assist Group Walking Mindfulness Meditation](https://doi.org/10.1145/3401956.3404240). MOCO '20.
- Cochrane, Loke, Leete, Campbell and Ahmadpour. [Understanding the First Person Experience of Walking Mindfulness Meditation Facilitated by EEG Modulated Interactive Soundscape](https://doi.org/10.1145/3430524.3440637). TEI '21.
